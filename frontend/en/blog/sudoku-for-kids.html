Sudoku for kids: benefits, rules and how to start with 4×4

By Ricardo Georgel · November 21, 2026 · 4 min read

Sudoku looks like a number game, but there's no arithmetic in it at all: the numbers are just symbols, and you could play with colors or pictures. What it trains is pure logical reasoning: observing, ruling out possibilities and being sure before writing anything down. That's why it works so well with children, as long as you start with the right size.

Where sudoku comes from

Despite the Japanese name, the modern version of the game was created in the United States and published in the late 1970s under the name "Number Place". A Japanese publisher took it to Japan in the 1980s and gave it the name sudoku, something like "the digits must be single". In the 2000s it spread around the world through newspapers and magazines.

What sudoku develops

  • Deductive reasoning. "If the 2 is already in this row and this column, this can only be the 4." It's the same logic used to solve problems and, later on, in math proofs.
  • Attention and focus. One number in the wrong place ruins the rest. The child learns to check before writing.
  • Persistence. Getting stuck is part of it. Learning to look for another clue instead of giving up helps with much more than the game.
  • Organization. Over time, the child develops a method: look first where there are more numbers, note down possibilities and eliminate.

Brazil's national curriculum (BNCC) doesn't mention sudoku, but it exercises what it calls computational thinking (recognizing patterns and following rules step by step) and helps with math skills such as finding the missing number that makes an equation true.

The rules, explained for children

In 4×4 sudoku, the grid has 4 rows, 4 columns and 4 small 2×2 boxes. You have to fill it with the numbers 1 to 4 so that:

  1. each row has 1, 2, 3 and 4, with no repeats;
  2. each column has 1, 2, 3 and 4, with no repeats;
  3. each small 2×2 box has 1, 2, 3 and 4, with no repeats.

A well-made sudoku has a single solution, and you can reach it with logic alone, without guessing.

One way to think about it:

  1. The first row is missing 1 and 3. The first column already has a 3 (in the second row), so the top left corner is 1, and the empty cell left in the row is 3.
  2. The second row is missing 2 and 4. The second column already has the 2 (at the top), so that cell gets the 4, and the last cell gets the 2.
  3. The third row is missing 2 and 4. The first column already has the 4 (at the bottom), so that cell gets the 2 and the other cell gets the 4.
  4. The last row has 3 and 1 left. Check the columns: the 3 goes in the second column and the 1 in the corner.
See the solution

1 2 3 4 · 3 4 1 2 · 2 1 4 3 · 4 3 2 1

Notice that in every step the question was the same: "what's missing here, and what's already ruled out?". Teaching that question is more important than solving the sudoku.

Why start with 4×4

The classic 9×9 sudoku has 81 cells and is too much for a beginner: the child gets lost, guesses and loses heart. The progression that works is:

  • 4×4 (numbers 1 to 4): from age 6 or 7. It can be solved in a few minutes and makes the rules clear.
  • 6×6 (numbers 1 to 6, 2×3 boxes): when 4×4 becomes easy. It requires noting down possibilities.
  • 9×9: after 6×6, starting with the "easy" levels in magazines and apps.

Tips for adults

  • Solve together the first few times and think out loud: "the top row has 2 and 4, so what's missing is…".
  • Use pencil and eraser. Making mistakes and erasing is part of it, and writing small numbers in the corner of a cell is a real technique.
  • For younger children, swap numbers for colors or pictures. The logic is the same.
  • Ask "how do you know?" when the child writes a number. It prevents guessing and trains justification.
  • Don't time it at first. Speed comes on its own with practice.
