Many people remember times tables as a list to recite until it sticks. Knowing the answers by heart is useful, because it frees the mind for bigger problems. But the road there is much easier when the child understands what they are multiplying and uses strategies. This guide shows the order that works and simple activities to do at home.

Meaning first, speed later

Before any times table, the child needs to understand that multiplying means putting equal groups together: 3 × 4 is 3 groups of 4. Brazil's national curriculum (BNCC) starts there in Grade 2, with multiplication by 2, 3, 4 and 5 as repeated addition (skill EF02MA07). In Grade 3 come arrays in rows and columns and building the basic facts (EF03MA03 and EF03MA07). The full times tables are usually consolidated between Grades 3 and 4.

If the child doesn't yet understand "groups of", memorizing only postpones the problem: the first time something differs from the list, they get stuck.

The strategies that shorten the times tables

The 1 to 10 times tables have 100 facts, but nobody needs to memorize 100 separate things. These ideas do almost all the work:

  • Order doesn't matter. 3 × 7 is the same as 7 × 3. Show it with a rectangle of dots that you can turn around. That alone removes almost half the facts.
  • × 1 and × 10. Multiplying by 1 doesn't change the number. Multiplying by 10 adds a zero at the end.
  • × 2 is double. And the child already knows doubles from addition: 7 + 7.
  • × 4 is double the double. 4 × 6: double 6 is 12, and double 12 is 24.
  • × 8 is double the × 4. 8 × 6 is double 24, that is, 48.
  • × 5 is half of × 10. 5 × 8: 10 × 8 = 80, and half of it is 40.
  • × 3 is × 2 plus one more group. 3 × 7 = 14 + 7 = 21.
  • × 6 is × 5 plus one more group. 6 × 7 = 35 + 7 = 42.
  • × 9 is × 10 minus one group. 9 × 7 = 70 − 7 = 63. Notice also that the digits of the answer add up to 9 (6 + 3).

Do the math: a child who masters × 1, × 2, × 5, × 9 and × 10 and knows that order doesn't matter has only 15 facts left to practice, all with the numbers 3, 4, 6, 7 and 8. Among them, 7 × 8 = 56 has a nice trick to remember it: 5, 6, 7, 8.

6 activities to do at home

1. Groups with objects

Use beans, bottle caps or blocks. Ask "make 4 groups of 3" and then "how many are there in all?". Next, arrange the same objects in 4 rows of 3: that's the array, which later becomes the idea of area.

2. Skip counting on the hundred chart

Print or draw a chart from 1 to 100. Color every 3rd number (3, 6, 9…) and look at the pattern that appears. Do the same with other colors for 4, 5 and 9. The child discovers patterns on their own, for example that multiples of 5 end in 0 or 5.

3. Multiplication war with a deck of cards

Take the face cards out of the deck (the ace is worth 1). Each player turns over two cards and multiplies. Whoever has the biggest result takes the cards. To start, use only cards up to 5. It's quick, fun, and every round has a different fact.

4. Two dice

Roll two dice and multiply. With regular dice, the child practices up to 6 × 6. A variation is to tally the results in a table and see which come up most often, which is already a first idea of probability.

5. Build the multiplication table

Draw a 10 × 10 grid with the numbers 1 to 10 across the top row and down the first column, and ask the child to fill it in, starting with the easiest rows (1, 2, 5, 10). At the end, ask them to look for patterns: the table is a "mirror" across the diagonal (that's order not mattering), and the row for 4 is double the row for 2.

6. Flashcards in three boxes

Write the hard facts on cards, with the answer on the back, and use three small boxes. Every card starts in box 1. Right answer, it moves up a box; wrong, it goes back to box 1. Practice box 1 every day, box 2 every other day, and box 3 once a week. That way the child spends time on what they don't know yet, and what they already know comes back now and then so it isn't forgotten.

How much time per day?

A little and often. Five to ten minutes a day pay off more than an hour on Saturday, because each time you come back the memory gets stronger. And when the child makes a mistake, go back to the strategy ("how can you work out 6 × 7 from 5 × 7?") instead of just giving the answer.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with speed. Use a timer only after the child gets it right calmly.
  • Always practicing the times tables in the same order. The child memorizes the sequence ("3, 6, 9, 12…") and not the individual fact. Mix up the facts.
  • Skipping division. As soon as the child knows 6 × 7, ask what 42 ÷ 7 is. One fact helps you remember the other.
